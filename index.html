Research

Grassroots Social Networking

Governments shut the Internet down during protests, and disasters cut it physically; people then turn to peer-to-peer messaging. Grassroots Social Networking is a peer-to-peer overlay for messaging and social networking that runs strictly on the smartphones of its users, with no servers, relays or shared ledgers. One application-layer overlay sits on two underlays: a BLE mesh between devices in radio range, and, when an Internet path exists, direct NAT-traversed streams between friends. Messages are relayed along the social graph only.

My thesis measures BLE link formation and multi-hop delivery on a testbed of eight commodity smartphones, and uses those measurements to parameterize simulations of the system at scale. BLE alone cannot carry a grassroots social network at scale: delivery is bounded by the radio airtime a node shares among its active links, not by range, and a second Internet underlay that bridges parts of the mesh is needed.

Joint research between the Technion, TU Munich and the Weizmann Institute.

Transport & protocol
BLE mesh underlay and an Internet underlay carrying UDX streams; NAT hole-punching and a rendezvous protocol; UDX against QUIC trade-offs; store-carry-forward buffering; relay restricted to the social graph.
Security
Public-key identities; Noise XX sessions over Curve25519 with ChaCha20-Poly1305.
Implementation
UDX, a reliable stream protocol implemented on top of UDP, as the Internet transport; a libp2p-style bootstrap node on GCP as a grassroots rendezvous server.
Evaluation
A testbed of eight smartphones across hardware classes, with tracing instruments for link formation and clique delivery under contention; simulations of link-establishment and packet-forwarding rules, parameterized by the measured link distributions.
Current work
A NAT topology and hole-punching survey: libp2p's AutoNAT in a grassroots approach, using multiple connected friends to characterize the NAT a peer sits behind.

Setup. Hall sets the square's side. A cluster is one cell of it and the radio reaches one cell side, so Cell sets the cluster size, the range and how many clusters the hall divides into at once — which is also why every member can hear into a neighbouring cluster. The seed fixes the topology and the sender–recipient pair, so switching rules repeats the same delivery, paired per seed. Speed is wall-clock only — a shorter tick runs the same mesh faster, and the congested rules need it.

Scope. The mesh carries background traffic at all times, with custody capped at 60 packets, hop budget 7, and idle link rotation for top-3 — the only rule that runs with the policy on. A node drives all its links at once and its radio's rate is split across the ones transferring, on top of a coarse contention penalty. Our message is injected into that running mesh. Suitability score

Every peer advertises a score for itself. A node ranks its links by the score the peer on the far side advertises — it never computes anything about that peer itself.

Sk= α⁢Ck +β⁢ (1− ρk)
Ck
Centrality: distinct neighbors that completed a transfer to k within the window W, over the link cap. Ck=min (1, fk(W) Dmax)
ρk
Buffer occupancy of k, as a fraction of its custody store. ρk= B−BfreeB

Gate and lanes

A link is a lane if its peer passes the gate. The node floods each message on its top F lanes only; messages addressed to a link's own peer bypass lanes entirely.

Lk= top-F { j∈N(k) :Sj≥θ }

On the wire

Both terms ride the peer's ANNOUNCE, quantized to one byte and refreshed once per announce epoch, so every decision runs on values as stale as that interval. Quantization is load-bearing: with raw doubles, jitter in ρ reshuffles near-tied rankings on every announce, and the resulting preemption storm aborts transfers mesh-wide.

Measures. Relays per delivered message is our airtime proxy, listed at 480 nodes. A shortest path costs its hop count, so the oracle stays at 8.2 → 10.0 across a threefold rise in node count while the flood goes 116 → 371: flooding costs O(n) per message, directed forwarding O(hops). Read the flood's median latency against its delivery — it falls with density only because the slow messages stop arriving at all.

Threats to validity. The simulator keeps an exact set of seen packet ids per peer; the implementation uses a Bloom filter of 96,000 bits over at most 10,000 items at ~1% false positives, rotating every 5 minutes. The modelled seen-set never errs, never fills and never forgets, so the sync exchange is cheaper here than on a device. The two errors do not cancel: a false positive skips a packet the node lacks, costing delivery where copies are fewest, while rotation forgets and old packets are reflooded, costing airtime where copies are most. We report relays per delivered as a lower bound, for every rule.
